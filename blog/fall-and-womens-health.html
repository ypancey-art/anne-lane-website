How Fall Affects Women's Bodies, Hormones & Mood

By Anne Lane · November 8, 2026 · 4 min read

Every year, as the clocks change and the evenings draw in, women arrive in my practice saying the same things: "I was fine all summer." "I'm sleeping badly." "Everything just got harder in October." They are not imagining it. Fall is a genuine physiological transition, and many women feel it more than they expect — especially in perimenopause or after a long stretch of stress.

Photos: Виктория Билан (left) and Thắng Văn (right) / Pexels

It starts with light

The biggest change in fall is the daylight reaching your eyes. Light sets your internal clock and influences melatonin (sleep), serotonin (mood), and cortisol (stress and wake-up). As mornings darken, waking feels heavier, the afternoon slump deepens, and sleep becomes less reliable. Less sun on the skin also means lower vitamin D, which supports immunity, mood, and bone health.

Photos: Yulia Polyakova (left) and Eddson Lens (right) / Pexels

Why women often feel it more

Seasonal low mood is diagnosed more often in women than in men. Estrogen and progesterone both interact with serotonin and the sleep-wake cycle, which may make women more sensitive to changes in light. Add the back-to-routine rush of September, and fall can feel like a full plate arriving just as your energy dips.

What I see most often in autumn

1. Disrupted sleep

Later sunrises shift melatonin timing. In perimenopause, when falling progesterone already makes sleep lighter, a nudge to the body clock can turn "slightly restless" into "awake at 3 a.m."

2. The fall slump

A mild dip in mood and drive as daylight shortens is very common. For some women it becomes seasonal affective disorder, with persistent low mood, heavy fatigue, and loss of interest — and that deserves proper medical support.

3. Cravings for carbohydrates and comfort food

When serotonin dips, the body asks for carbohydrates. That is not a lack of willpower, but the resulting blood sugar swings feed afternoon crashes and poor sleep. Protein, fibre, and regular meals help.

4. A more reactive immune system

Cooler weather, time indoors, and lower vitamin D converge. Women who are run down or short on key nutrients catch everything going around, so build resilience early.

5. Dry skin, cold hands, low energy

Indoor heating dries skin, lips, and eyes, and declining estrogen can amplify it. If you feel the cold far more than others, ask about iron and thyroid function rather than blaming the weather.

A different lens: autumn in Chinese medicine

In Traditional Chinese Medicine, autumn is the season of the Metal element, linked to the Lungs and Large Intestine. It is a time of contraction and letting go, when dryness can bring coughs, dry throats, and skin issues. The message is a sensible one: simplify, rest more, eat warm, nourishing foods, and don't try to hold a summer pace through the darkest months.

How to support yourself through fall

These are good starting points. If you would like a plan built around your own hormones, lab results, and history, that is what my 1:1 consultations are for.

Get light early, and get it daily

Spend 15–30 minutes outdoors within an hour of waking, even when it's cloudy. Outdoor light is far brighter than indoor light and anchors your body clock.

Check your vitamin D rather than guessing

A simple blood test shows where you stand. Supplement based on your result, ideally with a practitioner's guidance.

Eat to steady your blood sugar

Build meals around protein, healthy fats, and vegetables, and favour warm foods like soups, stews, and porridge.

Protect your sleep as the days shorten

Keep a consistent bedtime, dim the lights in the evening, and put screens away for the last hour. A cool, dark bedroom helps with night sweats.

Keep moving, gently

Walking outdoors supports mood and sleep and adds daylight. Consistency matters more than intensity.

When to seek support

A seasonal dip is normal. Low mood that lasts for weeks, makes daily life hard, or comes with hopelessness is not something to tough out — please speak to your doctor, and if you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, contact emergency services or a crisis line right away. If symptoms return every year, it's worth checking thyroid function, iron, vitamin D, B12, and hormones.
